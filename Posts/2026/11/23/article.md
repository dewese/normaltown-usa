# Bitcoin Taxes Without the Headache

> In the US, buying bitcoin with dollars and holding it isn't a taxable event. Selling it, spending it, or trading it can be, because the IRS treats bitcoin as property, not money. Hold it more than a year before you sell and any gain counts as long-term, which generally gets a lower rate than short-term. Keep good records, and talk to a tax pro about your own return.

Tax talk makes most people's eyes glaze over. Mine too. So here's the short, plain version of what I learned, without a single tax rate in it. Rates and limits change, and your situation isn't mine. That's what a tax pro is for.

## Bitcoin is taxed like a baseball card, not like a dollar

This is the one idea that makes the rest click.

The IRS says digital assets like bitcoin are property, not currency. So think of a baseball card, or a guitar, or anything else you might buy and later sell.

If you buy a baseball card for $10 and just keep it in a binder, you owe nothing. Owning stuff isn't taxed.

If you sell that card later for $50, you made $40. That $40 is a **capital gain**, which just means profit from selling something you owned. That's what gets taxed.

If you sell it for $4, you have a $6 **capital loss**. Losses can count too, usually against your gains.

Bitcoin works the same way.

## What usually counts and what doesn't

**Usually not a taxable event:**

- Buying bitcoin with dollars and holding it.
- Moving bitcoin between wallets or accounts that you own. The IRS says that's not a transaction, with one wrinkle: if you pay the network fee in bitcoin, that little fee piece can count. My weekly buys and River's [auto-withdrawals](/p/bitcoin-auto-withdrawals/) to my own hardware wallet fall in this bucket, apart from that fee wrinkle.

**Usually a taxable event:**

- Selling bitcoin for dollars.
- Spending bitcoin on something, like paying a bill. To the IRS, that's selling it and buying the thing.
- Trading bitcoin for another coin.
- Getting paid in bitcoin, or earning bitcoin as interest. That's generally income.

## The one-year line

When you sell, how long you held it matters.

Held **one year or less**: that's a short-term gain. It's generally taxed like your regular income.

Held **more than one year**: that's a long-term gain. The IRS says a lower rate may apply.

Here's the part that trips people up. If you buy every week like I do, every buy has its own clock. Your bitcoin isn't one pile with one date. It's a stack of little piles, each with its own purchase price and its own date. Those are called **tax lots**. Your purchase price on each one is your **cost basis**.

## The honest part

I'm not a tax pro, and this isn't tax advice. The rules have exceptions, and they change.

Keep records from day one. Every buy, every sell, every send. A spreadsheet is fine. Future you will thank present you.

There's also a yes or no question about digital assets near the top of the federal tax return. If you only bought and held, the IRS says the answer is generally no. If you sold, spent, or earned bitcoin, it's yes.

And taxes are only one way bitcoin can cost you. The price swings hard and can stay down for years. Selling in a panic during a drop locks in a loss, and that's a worse day than tax day. ## Questions I get about this

### Do I owe taxes if I buy bitcoin and never sell?

Generally, no. Buying with dollars and holding isn't a taxable event. Selling, spending, or trading is where tax usually comes in.

### Is sending bitcoin to my own hardware wallet taxable?

The IRS says moving digital assets between wallets you own isn't a transaction, unless you paid the network fee in bitcoin, which can count. Talk to a tax pro about how to handle those fees.

### What records should I keep?

The date, amount, and price of every buy and sell, and every send between wallets. River's Performance page and Taxes & Documents section cover what happened on River. You track the rest.
