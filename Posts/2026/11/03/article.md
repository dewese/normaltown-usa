# You Don't Need a Whole Bitcoin: Sats, Explained

> One bitcoin splits into 100 million tiny pieces called satoshis, or "sats." You can buy $10 worth and own real bitcoin, just a small slice of one. The price of a whole coin sounds huge, but almost nobody buys a whole one, and you don't need to.

The most common thing I hear from friends is some version of "I can't afford a bitcoin." And that's true for most of us. It's also beside the point. You were never supposed to buy a whole one.

## The short answer

Every bitcoin can be split into 100,000,000 smaller units. Each unit is called a satoshi, or a "sat" for short. It's named after Satoshi Nakamoto, the made-up name used by whoever created bitcoin.

So one sat is 0.00000001 bitcoin. That's a decimal point followed by seven zeros and a one. Tiny.

When you buy $10 of bitcoin, you're buying a pile of sats. It's real bitcoin. It's just not a whole coin.

## Pennies and dollars

You already do this with the dollar. Nobody says "I can't afford a dollar, so I'll skip the gumball machine." You put in a quarter. A quarter is part of a dollar, and nobody thinks it's fake money.

Sats are the same idea, just sliced much thinner. A dollar breaks into 100 cents. A bitcoin breaks into 100 million sats.

## Why the whole-coin price throws people off

When the news says "bitcoin hits some big number," that's the price of one whole coin. It sounds like a price tag, like you need that much to get in the door.

It's really just a rate. You can buy any slice of a coin, the same way you can spend a quarter without handing over a whole dollar.

So the question isn't "can I afford one bitcoin?" It's "how many dollars do I want to set aside?" Ten dollars buys ten dollars of bitcoin, no matter what a whole coin costs that day.

## Why some people count in sats

Because a whole coin is expensive, the decimals get long and clumsy. Saying "I have 0.0002 bitcoin" is a mouthful. Saying "I have 20,000 sats" is easier to read and easier to compare.

You'll see sats used for small amounts in a lot of bitcoin apps. River Learn's lesson points out that bitcoin's own code keeps track of everything in sats behind the scenes. "Bitcoin" is just the friendly name for 100 million of them.

There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. Multiply that by 100 million and you get 2.1 quadrillion sats. That's the whole supply, forever. If you want the longer version of why a fixed supply matters, I wrote about [what scarce means for your money](/p/what-scarce-means-for-your-money/).

## The honest part

Owning a small amount doesn't make it a small risk. The price of bitcoin swings hard. Your $10 of sats could be worth $6 next month, and bitcoin has spent years at a time well below an old high. Nobody can promise you a return.

So the rule doesn't change just because the amount is small: only buy with money you can leave alone. Small is a great way to learn. I walked through [buying your first $20](/p/your-first-20-in-bitcoin/) for exactly that reason.
