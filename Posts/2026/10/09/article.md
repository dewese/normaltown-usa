# The $240,000 NICU Bill

> A family's baby was born with Down syndrome, spent 18 or 19 days in the NICU, and later had open-heart surgery. The baby's part of the hospital bill was over $240,000. The dad called CrowdHealth from the parking lot asking if he was in trouble. He wasn't. The bills were negotiated and funded, with some real bumps along the way.

This is the biggest kind of bill a young family can get. So I wanted to see how it went for someone.

## What happened?

A dad told this story in a video and again in a CrowdHealth webinar this September. He'd joined years earlier because the insurance at his small company was poor. Before this, his family went to the doctor about once a year.

Their baby boy was born by C-section, struggling to breathe, and went straight to the NICU. That's the intensive care unit for newborns. He has Down syndrome, and he had two holes in his heart.

He was in the NICU for 18 or 19 days. At seven and a half months old, he had open-heart surgery.

## What did the bill look like?

The baby's share of the hospital bill alone was over $240,000. The doctors' group sent a separate bill on top of that.

The dad said he sat in the hospital parking lot, called CrowdHealth, and asked, more or less, whether he was in trouble.

They told him no. They told him which papers to send. The bills went out to the crowd and got funded.

There's a part I didn't expect. After the bills were paid, CrowdHealth kept negotiating. The hospital ended up giving a bigger discount than planned, and that money went back to the members who had chipped in.

## What did CrowdHealth actually do?

Three things: told a scared dad what to do next, negotiated a quarter-million-dollar bill, and split what was left across a whole lot of members so no one person felt it.

That last part is the heart of it. A $240,000 bill would flatten one family. Split into small pieces across a big crowd, nobody gets flattened. It's like a barn raising. One family can't lift a barn wall. Forty neighbors can do it before lunch.

## What's the honest part?

The dad said it himself, and I respect him for it. In his words, "we kind of broke CrowdHealth."

This was early in the company's life. The app wasn't built to handle that many bills from one family. Uploading them was a mess. He got on a call with someone there and told them what needed fixing. He says later versions of the app reflected it. The founder agreed they hadn't worked out all the kinks for huge bills yet.

The parents also described a stretch of real fear before they knew it would be funded. They wondered if joining had been a mistake. It worked out. They didn't know that while they were living it.

## The takeaway

A $240,000 NICU bill got negotiated and funded, and extra savings went back to the crowd. It also strained a young company's systems, and the family felt that. Both things are true. I'd rather be in a crowd that admits where it struggled than one that pretends it never does.

## Questions I get about this

### Has CrowdHealth funded really large bills?

Yes. CrowdHealth publishes a list of its largest funded bills. The top ones are $643,242, $437,729, and $333,943, and four of the top 25 are NICU stays. It says there's no yearly or lifetime cap on eligible bills. Funding still isn't guaranteed. See [can you trust health sharing with a big bill](/p/can-you-trust-health-sharing-with-a-big-bill/).

### Does a long illness reset my $500 every year?

No. A health event is everything tied to one condition, and CrowdHealth says it doesn't reset each January. CrowdHealth's founder described one member's cancer, with two surgeries and ongoing treatment, as a single $500 event.

### What if a condition is found before birth?

The pregnancy has to be eligible, meaning a due date at least 300 days after joining. A baby's complications at birth are then a separate $500 event. Conditions a person had before joining fall under the pre-existing rule, which I explain in [pre-existing conditions and health sharing](/p/pre-existing-conditions-and-health-sharing/).
