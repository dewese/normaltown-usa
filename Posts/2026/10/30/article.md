# What the Critics Say About CrowdHealth (and Where I Think They're Right)

> Health policy experts have called CrowdHealth "rolling the dice" and warned that models like it work until they don't. Their main points: payment isn't guaranteed, no regulator can help if a bill goes unpaid, long-term illness is a weak spot, and healthy-member rules resemble old-style insurance. I agree with several of these. Here's each one, with my honest take.

I've spent most of a month telling you why I'm a fan. A fan who can't repeat the other side's best argument isn't worth listening to. So here it is.

## What do the critics say?

I pulled these from national magazines, a business magazine, a regional newspaper, and a state insurance agency's consumer warning.

**It works until it doesn't.** A health policy executive told a business magazine that a few unlucky, very expensive cases could overwhelm a small pool of members. He also said it would probably work for most members, while the risk to any one person could be high.

**It's a gamble.** A university researcher called it "rolling the dice" in a newspaper story this spring. A consumer advocate in the same story said plans like this work for routine care until something serious happens.

**No one can step in.** One state's insurance agency put out a consumer warning that named CrowdHealth. Its points: it isn't insurance, payment isn't guaranteed, the usual legal protections for insurance customers don't apply, and the state can't step in on a complaint.

**The statistic is vague.** A national magazine noted that CrowdHealth's funding percentage doesn't spell out exactly what gets counted.

**It looks like old-style insurance.** Critics point out that screening people by age, tobacco use, weight, and past conditions is what insurers did before the law stopped them. One added that the model partly leans on members being able to go buy a regular plan if they get seriously sick.

**Long-term care is the hole.** A writer who joined CrowdHealth himself, and likes it, laid this out best. A condition that needs expensive treatment forever is the real danger, because ongoing prescriptions are limited to a year. He also doubted the crowd could absorb several million-dollar bills in a single month. For what it's worth, CrowdHealth told members in October 2026 that the crowd had recently taken on events of nearly $3 million and $1.3 million, and that the monthly ask had ticked up as it usually does late in the year.

## Where do I think they're right?

**The guarantee.** They're right. Nobody is legally required to pay my bill. I've said that in every post this month. The track record is strong. A track record isn't a contract.

**No regulator.** Right again. If CrowdHealth denied a bill of mine and I thought they were wrong, there's no state office to call. I'd be relying on their own review and their reputation.

**The long-term illness gap.** This is the one I take most seriously. A sudden event, even a huge one, is what the crowd handles well. A lifelong condition needing costly medicine every month is where it's thin. Read the member guide's section on prescriptions before you rely on it.

**The fuzzy statistic.** Fair. "99% of eligible bills" depends a lot on the word "eligible." That's why I've tried to give you the other numbers too: 3% to 5% of submitted bills ruled ineligible each month, and a small number of flagged requests left unfunded or partly funded.

## Where do I push back?

**The small pool.** That warning is from early 2024. CrowdHealth has funded more than 53,000 bills since it started, so it's bigger than it was. It's still tiny next to an insurance company, and the worry isn't gone.

**The comparison.** Critics often compare CrowdHealth to good insurance. For a lot of normal families, that isn't the choice. The choice is between CrowdHealth and a plan with a huge monthly bill and a deductible so high they'd pay for nearly everything themselves anyway. One family in that newspaper story was looking at their monthly insurance bill going from $600 to $2,000.

**The transparency.** Monthly results, a list of denied bills with reasons, and a public complaints log. I haven't seen an insurer publish that. It doesn't make the risk go away. It does let me watch it.

Here's my analogy. Insurance is a contract with a company that's motivated to say no. CrowdHealth is a handshake with a company that's motivated to say yes. I'd rather have a good handshake than a bad contract. But I know it's a handshake.

## The takeaway

The critics aren't wrong about the risk. They're describing it accurately. Where we differ is on what the alternative looks like for a healthy family paying full price for insurance. If you need a legal guarantee, or you have an ongoing condition, listen to the critics and keep real insurance. That's not a failure. That's the right tool for you.

## Questions I get about this

### Is CrowdHealth regulated like insurance?

No. It isn't insurance, so state insurance departments don't oversee it and can't help with a complaint. At least one state agency has issued a consumer warning saying so. I cover the legal side in [is CrowdHealth legit](/p/is-crowdhealth-legit/).

### What's the biggest risk with CrowdHealth?

A serious condition that needs costly care for years. Ongoing prescriptions are limited to a 12-month supply, and funding is never guaranteed. Sudden events are what the model handles best. See [who health sharing is wrong for](/p/who-health-sharing-is-wrong-for/).

### Can I keep insurance and have CrowdHealth too?

Yes. CrowdHealth's founder says about 15% to 20% of members also keep insurance. That costs more each month, and it keeps a legal guarantee in place.
