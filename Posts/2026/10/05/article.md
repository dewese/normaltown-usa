# A Toddler, a Medevac, and $30,000 in Bills

> A dad's 18-month-old son couldn't breathe and was medevacked to a pediatric ICU 90 minutes away. The bills came to more than $30,000. CrowdHealth helped negotiate them down by more than half, the crowd shared what was left, and the dad paid his $500. A couple of bills were still open when he wrote about it.

This is the story that made me want to write this whole series. It's every parent's bad night.

## What happened?

A dad wrote a public review earlier this year about his 18-month-old son. The boy had a rough cough and started struggling to breathe. They rushed to the ER in the middle of the night. After about 12 hours on oxygen, the doctors said he needed a pediatric ICU, and it was an hour and a half away. So he went by medevac.

The dad says he asked along the way what all this would cost. He was told not to worry about it. Any parent would have said yes to that ride anyway.

His son got better. Then the mail started coming.

## What did the bills look like?

More than $30,000 worth, in several envelopes. That's how hospital billing works. The ER sends one. The medevac company sends one. The ICU sends one. The doctors send their own. It's like a restaurant where the cook, the waiter, and the dishwasher each mail you a separate check.

Here's how the dad says it shook out:

- **Billed:** over $30,000
- **After negotiating:** cut by more than 50%
- **What he paid:** his $500 member commitment
- **The rest:** shared by other members

He also said some of the big bills were paid back to him before his own payment to the hospital was even due.

## What did CrowdHealth actually do?

Two things. First, they negotiated. A hospital's first bill is a sticker price, like the number on a new car window. Almost nobody pays it. Insurance companies don't. Cash payers shouldn't either. I wrote about that in [the cash price secret](/p/the-cash-price-secret/).

Second, once the price was fair, they sent the bills out to the crowd. Other members each chipped in a small amount, and the money landed in his account.

He also mentioned something that shows up in a lot of member reviews. When he had a "what if" question, a real person answered the same day or the next. No hold music.

## What's the honest part?

A few things, and the dad said most of them himself.

It wasn't finished. A couple of bills were still being worked on two months later. Big events take a while to close out.

You are the one holding the bills while that happens. Your name is on them, not CrowdHealth's.

And his situation fit the rules. A sudden illness in a healthy kid is exactly what this is built for. He listed the limits in his own review: conditions you already had aren't shared for the first two years, you have to join before a pregnancy, and people 65 and up or who use tobacco can't join.

## Why this one sticks with me

His old insurance plan cost about $500 a month just for him, with a $4,000 deductible. He figured a family plan would have run about $1,500 a month with a $12,000 deductible. A deductible is the amount you pay yourself before insurance pays a dime. So on that plan, this same night could have cost him $12,000 on top of the monthly bill.

Instead it was $500. That's the whole pitch, in one bad night.

## The takeaway

When a kid can't breathe, nobody shops for prices. The shopping happens after, and this dad had someone doing it for him. More than $30,000 billed, cut by more than half, $500 out of pocket. Not finished yet when he wrote it, and not guaranteed. But it worked the way he was told it would.

## Questions I get about this

### Does CrowdHealth cover ambulance or helicopter rides?

In this member's story, the medevac was part of the health event and its bill was handled with the others. One limit I do know: CrowdHealth's member guide says international air ambulance is not eligible. Read the current guide, because it's the rulebook.

### Do I have to pay the hospital first and wait?

Often you pay and get paid back. For bigger bills, CrowdHealth says it can negotiate before you pay anything. This dad said some large bills were funded before his payment was due. I walk through the steps in [how one health event gets paid](/p/how-one-health-event-gets-paid/).

### What should I do in a real emergency?

Go. Get care first and sort out the money later. No plan of any kind is worth a delay when someone can't breathe. I wrote a step-by-step for this in [health sharing and the ER](/p/health-sharing-and-the-er/).
