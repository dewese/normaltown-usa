# Is CrowdHealth Legit? What I Found When I Checked

> Yes, in the sense that matters: it's a real company doing exactly what it says. 53,877 bills funded, 99.8 percent of submissions funded, 4.9 stars on Trustpilot, about 35,000 members. But "legit" and "insurance" are different questions. Nobody is legally required to fund your bill, and no state regulator can help you if they don't. That's the actual trade.

"Is this a scam?" is the first thing anybody asks me about CrowdHealth, and it's the right question to ask about anything that promises to replace a $1,500 premium.

So let's check it properly. I'm a member, my family of four has used it since 2022, and I earn a referral if you join through me. Weigh that, and then look at the evidence yourself.

## The short answer

CrowdHealth is a real, operating company that does what it describes. It isn't a scam.

It's also not insurance, doesn't claim to be, and doesn't carry the protections insurance carries. Both of those things are true at once, and the second one is the part that should actually drive your decision.

## The track record

Numbers first, because they're checkable.

- **53,877 bills funded.** That's a lot of real money moving to real people.
- **99.8 percent of submitted bills get funded.**
- **About 35,000 members.**
- **4.9 stars on Trustpilot.**
- **About $100 million** saved versus traditional insurance, which is CrowdHealth's own figure and should be read as a company claim, not an audit.

Sit with the first and third for a second. Fifty-three thousand bills across about thirty-five thousand members is **about three funded bills for every two members**. That's not a program where a handful of people use it and everybody else just pays in. The crowd is actually working.

## What "legit" doesn't mean

Here's where most reviews stop, and where I think they do you a disservice.

A strong track record is evidence about the past. It's not a contract about your future. The distinction matters more than anything else on this page, so let me put it plainly.

**When you buy insurance, you're buying two things: the money, and the referee.**

The referee is a state agency with the power to make a company pay you. You never think about it until you need it, and most people never need it. But it exists.

CrowdHealth has the money part, and the record above says the money part works. **It does not have the referee.** There's no legal obligation for anybody to fund your bill, and there's no government office to call if they don't.

That's not a loophole somebody discovered. It's stated plainly in the actual terms, and it's the trade you're making. The cost is lower partly *because* that machinery isn't there.

Maryland's insurance regulator [published a consumer advisory about CrowdHealth](https://insurance.maryland.gov/Consumer/Pages/ConsumerAdvisory-MIA-warns-of-risks-involved-in-crowd-funding-healthcare-payment-platform-CrowdHealth.aspx) in March 2026 making essentially this point. I'd rather you read it yourself than take my summary or the company's.

## It isn't a health sharing ministry either

Worth knowing, because it changes how you read other reviews.

Most comparison sites file CrowdHealth under "health sharing plans" next to the faith-based programs. CrowdHealth's own term for itself is **healthcare crowdfunding**, and it's not a ministry: it's for-profit, and there's no shared-beliefs requirement.

That's not trivia. Many states carve out a legal exception specifically for sharing ministries on religious grounds. CrowdHealth doesn't get that carve-out, which is exactly what Maryland's notice turns on. If a review treats it as a ministry, that review hasn't understood the product.

## How to check it yourself

Don't take my word for any of this. It takes twenty minutes:

1. **Read the member guide**, especially the sections on eligibility and conditions you already have. The limits are stated, not buried.
2. **Read Trustpilot,** and read the one-star reviews, not just the average. Look for whether complaints are about the rules being unfair or about the rules being hidden. Those are very different problems.
3. **Check your own state.** Some states have individual mandates that change the math. Mine that list yourself, because the version I have is out of date and I'd rather say so than hand you a stale list.
4. **Ask them a hard question in writing** before you join, about your specific situation, and see how they answer.

## Questions I get about this

### Is CrowdHealth a scam?

No. It's a real operating company with a long, checkable record: 53,877 bills funded, 99.8 percent of submissions funded, 4.9 stars on Trustpilot. The real question isn't whether it's a scam, it's whether you're comfortable without a legal guarantee.

### Is CrowdHealth insurance?

No, and it doesn't claim to be. It's healthcare crowdfunding. It doesn't satisfy the ACA's minimum essential coverage requirement, and it isn't regulated as insurance.

### What happens if CrowdHealth doesn't pay my bill?

You're responsible for it, and no state insurance department can intervene. The funding rate is 99.8 percent, but a rate is not a guarantee. [The longer answer is here](/p/what-if-the-crowd-doesnt-fund-your-bill/).

### Is CrowdHealth a health sharing ministry?

No. It's for-profit and secular, with no shared-beliefs requirement, so it doesn't qualify for the state exemptions written for sharing ministries.

### How many members does CrowdHealth have?

About 35,000 as of September 2026, according to CrowdHealth's CEO. That's still small next to an insurance company.

### Why did Maryland issue a warning about CrowdHealth?

Because it isn't insurance, doesn't guarantee payment, and doesn't fit Maryland's sharing-ministry exemption, so state regulators can't help a member whose bill goes unfunded. Read the advisory yourself, linked above.
