## What happened?

A woman whose first health event was a pregnancy wrote a four-star review. She had to get itemized bills, haggle with billing offices, pay up front, and then wait to be repaid. She said it has paid off compared with insurance. She also said she wished the load were lighter.

A different member, back in 2024, wrote that sorting out bills and payments for health events had become a part-time job.

Same phrase, in two separate reviews.

Others filled in the details:

- One member paid bills by mailed check and then had to print bank statements to prove the payments had cleared. Almost two months in, nothing had moved.
- One had to open an account at a separate bank just to receive the funds, and each bill came through as its own transfer.
- One said doctors' offices are so used to insurance that you have to explain yourself constantly.
- One long-time member has reread the member guide several times trying to work out the right steps.
- A blogger said it plainly: it's more hands-on than flashing an insurance card.

## What does the work actually look like?

Here's the routine, as members describe it.

**Before care, when you can:** open a health event in the app and tell your advocate. Ask the provider for the cash price in writing.

**At the visit:** say you're paying directly. Ask for an itemized bill, which lists every charge on its own line.

**After:** pay, or work out a plan. Upload the itemized bill and proof you paid. Then wait for it to be reviewed, negotiated if needed, and funded.

**The deadline:** bills have to be turned in within six months of the date of care.

It's a lot like filing your own taxes instead of having them taken out of each paycheck. You come out ahead. You also have to keep the shoebox of receipts.

## Has it gotten better?

Some. CrowdHealth says it rebuilt its app in late 2024, and many of the harshest paperwork reviews are from before that. The company also says the app now shows which step each bill is on.

A member who joined early said reimbursement used to be slow and came by mailed check. Now, they said, bills are approved in about a week and deposited about two days after.

But the complaints log CrowdHealth publishes still shows bumps this year. Members charged twice by mistake. Members who couldn't reach the banking page. A price shown in the app that didn't match what the provider charged.

## What's the honest part?

Insurance has paperwork too. Denials, appeals, surprise bills, letters that say "this is not a bill." The difference is who does the work. With insurance, most of it happens out of sight until something goes wrong. With CrowdHealth, it's yours from the first day.

Some people will hate that. If you have a spouse who already handles all the family paperwork and is stretched thin, be honest about who's going to do this. If you're in the middle of a health crisis, the last thing you want is homework.

Other people like it. One woman with a new cancer diagnosis said she felt she had more say in her own treatment, even with the extra legwork.

My take: nobody loves forms, but I do like knowing what things cost.

## The takeaway

The savings on CrowdHealth aren't free money. You're being paid, in a way, to do some of the work an insurance company's back office would do. For a lot of normal families, that's a great trade. Go in knowing it's a trade.

## Questions I get about this

### How hard is it to submit a bill to CrowdHealth?

You upload an itemized bill and proof of payment in the app. Simple bills are quick. A big event with many providers means many bills, and two members compared that to a part-time job. Start a folder the day something happens.

### How long do I have to turn in a bill?

Six months from the date of care, according to CrowdHealth's member guide. For planned care, open the event before the appointment. Waiting can affect whether a bill is eligible. I list more of these in [what to ask before you switch](/p/what-to-ask-before-you-switch/).

### What is an itemized bill?

It's a bill that lists every single charge on its own line, with a code for each one. It's how errors and overcharges get caught. Hospitals don't always send one unless you ask. Ask every time, on any plan.
