# The Small Stuff: When the Bill Is Under $500

> On CrowdHealth, anything under $500 for a health event is yours to pay. A member's daughter needed staples after a trampoline park fall, and the family paid all $175. Another member's knee visit was quoted over $800 and dropped to about $300 with a cash discount. A third paid $500 of an $850 testing bill and wasn't happy.

Most health stuff in a normal year is small. Stitches. A sprain. A weird rash. So what does a crowd do for you when the bill is $175? Nothing, mostly. And that's by design.

## What happened?

**The trampoline park.** A dad wrote on his company's blog that his daughter banged her head at a trampoline park and needed staples, plus a follow-up visit to take them out. All in, about $175.

They sent the bills to CrowdHealth anyway, in case the total passed $500. It didn't. So they paid the whole thing.

**The knee.** A self-employed writer's knee swelled up with fluid and needed to be drained. The first doctor didn't have the right equipment and waived the $457 visit fee. At the second office he was quoted more than $800. With a cash discount, he paid a little over $300.

Again, under $500. Nothing went to the crowd. He was pleased anyway, because he saved $500 or so just by being a cash patient with someone helping him find the right office.

**The testing bill.** This one's the unhappy version. A member had testing done that their doctor suggested and CrowdHealth okayed. They negotiated the price themselves. The bill was $850. They still had to pay $500 of it.

They gave three stars and said it made them doubt whether a real emergency would go any better.

CrowdHealth replied that on a plan with a deductible, they'd have paid the entire $850. The $500 is the trade for the lower monthly cost.

## What does the math look like?

On the $850 bill: member pays $500, crowd pays $350. That's 59% out of the member's pocket. I get why it stung.

On a $30,000 bill: member pays $500, crowd pays $29,500. That's under 2%.

Same rule both times. The $500 is a flat amount, so it's a big share of a small bill and a tiny share of a big one.

Think of a cover charge at the door. It's $10 whether you stay for one song or dance until they turn the lights on. If you leave after one song, $10 feels like a ripoff. If you stay all night, it was nothing.

## What's the honest part?

If your family's health costs are mostly small things, you'll pay for nearly all of them yourself. In a typical year for a healthy family, the crowd may not pay you a dime. You're paying each month for protection against the big one, and for the cash-price help on the small ones.

That means the right comparison isn't "what did the crowd pay me this year?" It's "what did I pay in total, and what would I have paid the other way?"

For the trampoline dad, the answer was good. He figures his family saves at least $600 a month compared with insurance. A $175 staple bill doesn't dent that.

For someone with a $500 commitment and four separate $400 events in one year, it's $1,600 out of pocket with nothing shared. That's a real scenario. Each event is its own $500, per person.

This is what a cushion is for. It's the same idea as [the first $1,000 emergency fund](/p/the-first-1000-emergency-fund/). The $500 isn't scary when it's already sitting there.

## The takeaway

The crowd is for the big bills. For the small ones, your win is the cash price, and it can be a good win: $300 instead of $800, $175 and done. Just don't expect a check for a $400 visit. Plan to cover the small stuff yourself and you'll never be surprised by it.

## Questions I get about this

### What happens if my bill is less than $500?

You pay all of it. The $500 member commitment applies to each health event, and nothing goes to the crowd until that event's bills pass $500. You still benefit from asking for cash prices, which can cut a small bill by half or more.

### Is the $500 per year or per event?

Per event, per person. A broken arm is one event. A separate ear infection is another. It doesn't reset in January, and it also doesn't cap out for the year. I compare this to a deductible in [the $500 you pay vs the deductible you don't](/p/the-500-you-pay-vs-the-deductible-you-dont/).

### Should I submit small bills anyway?

Yes. If follow-up visits push the event past $500, you'll want the earlier bills already on file. One family did exactly this for a child's staples, and it stayed under the line.
