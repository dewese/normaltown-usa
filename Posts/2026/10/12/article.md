# The $10,000 Bill the Crowd Never Saw

> A couple about seven months into CrowdHealth had a $10,000 bill denied. The reason was a symptom that had been checked by a doctor before they joined. Under CrowdHealth's rules, anything diagnosed, treated, or showing symptoms in the five years before joining isn't shared for two years, and is capped at $25,000 a year after that.

I promised the bad stories too. This is the first one, and it's the rule that trips up more people than any other.

## What happened?

A woman wrote a one-star public review about a claim for her household. They'd been members for almost seven months. They were paying about $110 a month in fees, plus $200 to $300 a month toward other members' bills.

Then they had a health problem that produced a $10,000 bill. CrowdHealth had them fill out forms describing the issue. In those forms was a symptom that had come up before, one a doctor had looked at before they joined.

The bill was denied. They appealed. The appeal was denied too. The bill was never sent to the crowd at all.

## Why was it denied?

Here is the rule, as plainly as I can put it.

If you had a condition in the five years before joining, it isn't shared during your first two years. In year three and after, it's shared up to $25,000 a year.

The part people miss is what "had a condition" means. It isn't only a diagnosis. It also covers symptoms, things a doctor suspected, and anything you were treated for. So a symptom that got looked at and never got a name can still count.

CrowdHealth replied publicly to her review. They said the rule is on the sign-up page and in the member guide, and they offered to help find a lower-cost way to get the care.

Think of it like buying a used car "as is." Anything already rattling when you drove it off the lot is yours to fix. The warranty is for what breaks later.

## How often does this happen?

More than I expected, and CrowdHealth publishes it. Each month, about 3% to 5% of submitted bills are ruled not eligible. In August 2026 it was 4.94%.

Their results list the reasons, and some are brutal in how small they are. One note says a member's symptoms started three days before their start date. Another says one month before. Another says medical records showed three weeks of symptoms before joining.

Another member wrote that an event of theirs was flagged as previously diagnosed when they say it wasn't, and it was still unresolved when they posted.

## What's the honest part?

I think the rule itself is fair. A crowd can't work if people join the week after they find a lump. Somebody has to pay for that, and it'd be the rest of us.

But fair and painless aren't the same thing. This couple thought they were covered and found out otherwise holding a $10,000 bill. Whether they misread the rule or it wasn't explained well, the result was the same for them.

To her credit, the reviewer added that CrowdHealth might still be a good fit for a pregnancy or a sudden emergency. That's about right. This is built for new things, not old things.

There are two exceptions worth knowing. High blood pressure and high cholesterol are exempt from the rule. And brand-new accidents and new illnesses are eligible from day one.

## The takeaway

Before you join, sit down and list every doctor visit and every nagging symptom from the last five years. Assume anything on that list is on you for two years. If that list has something expensive on it, CrowdHealth may not be your tool right now, and I'd tell you that to your face.

## Questions I get about this

### What counts as a pre-existing condition on CrowdHealth?

Anything diagnosed, treated, suspected, or showing symptoms in the five years before you joined. It doesn't need a formal diagnosis. Those conditions aren't shared in years one and two, then are capped at $25,000 a year. Full breakdown in [CrowdHealth and pre-existing conditions](/p/crowdhealth-pre-existing-conditions/).

### Can I appeal a denied bill?

You can ask for a review. In this member's case the appeal was also denied. CrowdHealth isn't insurance, so there's no state regulator to take it to afterward. That's a real difference, and I talk about it in [health sharing vs insurance](/p/health-sharing-vs-insurance-honest-trade-offs/).

### Who should not join because of this rule?

Anyone who needs steady, costly care for something they already have. A guaranteed insurance plan is the better fit for that person. I wrote the whole list in [who health sharing is wrong for](/p/who-health-sharing-is-wrong-for/).
