# How Much Does CrowdHealth Cost? The Real Numbers, With the Cap

> CrowdHealth costs $60 a month per person, children included, plus a crowd contribution that's hard-capped by age and household. Under 55 your worst possible month is $200. From 55 to 64 it's $340. For a family of four or more it's $660. On top of that you cover the first $500 of any health event. The crowd usually asks for less than the cap.

Every time somebody asks me what CrowdHealth costs, I give them a number, and then I watch them not believe it. So let me do this the long way, with the arithmetic in the open.

My family of four has been on it since 2022. Here's exactly how the money works.

## The three pieces

**One. The subscription: $60 a month, per person.**

Children count. A family of four pays $240 a month in subscription. This is the part that doesn't move. It pays for the app, the people who negotiate your bills down, and a Care Advocate you can actually reach.

It does not go up because you got older, and it does not go up because you got sick.

**Two. The crowd contribution: variable, but capped.**

Each month you chip in to fund other members' bills. That's the crowdfunding part, and it's the number people worry about, because "variable" sounds like "unlimited."

It isn't. There's a published ceiling.

**Three. The member commitment: $500 per health event.**

A health event is one thing that happens to you. A broken wrist. An appendix. You cover the first $500 of it, and the crowd covers the eligible rest. It works like a deductible, except it resets per event instead of per year, and it's $500 instead of five figures.

## The cap, which is the part nobody tells you

| Who you are | Most the crowd can ask in one month | Your worst case, with the $60 |
|---|---|---|
| Ages 0 to 54 | $140 | **$200 per person** |
| Ages 55 to 64 | $280 | **$340 per person** |
| A family of four or more | $420 for the whole household | **$660 for the family** |

Read the bottom row twice. A family of four pays $240 in subscription, and the crowd can ask for at most $420 more. **Six hundred sixty dollars is the worst month that family can possibly have.** Then $500 on top if somebody actually gets hurt that month.

That's the ceiling. It is not the normal month. CrowdHealth has historically not asked for the full amount, and in a quiet month the ask is a good bit lower.

## Let's do it for real people

**A 35-year-old on her own.** $60 subscription. Worst month, $200. If she breaks an ankle in March, add $500 that one time. Her worst possible year, assuming the crowd maxed her out every single month and she got hurt once, is $2,400 plus $500, so $2,900.

Now price a bronze marketplace plan for a 35-year-old without a subsidy. The premium alone often beats $2,900 before the deductible does one thing for her.

**A 58-year-old bridging to Medicare.** $60 subscription, but his cap is $280, so his worst month is $340 and his worst year is $4,080 plus commitments. That's a real step up from the under-55 number, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. Then go price an individual plan for a 58-year-old. It's frequently north of a thousand dollars a month. The gap is still enormous. More on that in [health sharing for early retirees](/p/health-sharing-for-early-retirees/).

**A family of four, two adults under 55.** $240 subscription. Worst month $660. Worst year $7,920, before any $500 commitments.

Compare that to a family plan with a $1,500 monthly premium and a $12,000 deductible, which is $18,000 before the plan pays for anything. That comparison is why we switched.

## Where the savings actually come from

It isn't magic, and it isn't the crowd being generous. Two things do the work.

**Cash prices.** When you walk in as self-pay instead of insured, the price is often dramatically lower for the identical service. Discounts commonly run 25 to 85 percent off billed charges. CrowdHealth's team negotiates this for you before the crowd ever sees the bill, which means the crowd is funding a smaller number. I wrote about the mechanism in [why cash prices beat insured prices](/p/why-cash-prices-beat-insured-prices/).

**Aligned incentives.** CrowdHealth makes its money on that flat $60, not on a slice of your claims. Nobody there gets a bonus for slow-walking your bill. That's the whole argument in [nobody gets paid to make you well](/p/nobody-gets-paid-to-make-you-well/).

## What the price does not buy you

I'd be selling you something if I stopped here.

The low number is low partly because you're giving something up, and it's a real thing. **There is no legal guarantee your bill gets funded.** CrowdHealth is not insurance. No state insurance regulator can step in for you if a bill goes unfunded.

The funding rate is 99.8 percent across 53,877 bills, which is a strong record. A record is not a promise, and I won't dress it up as one.

You're also not buying coverage for a condition you already have. Anything documented, diagnosed, or symptomatic within five years of joining gets nothing in years one and two, then up to $25,000 a year from year three. High cholesterol and high blood pressure are exempt. If that rule lands on you, the cheap monthly number is beside the point.

The full picture, including who should walk away, is on my [CrowdHealth guide](/crowdhealth/).

## So is it cheaper?

For a reasonably healthy household with a cushion, on our numbers, by a lot.

For somebody with an ongoing condition, or somebody getting a large ACA subsidy, quite possibly not. Run your own numbers before you cancel anything, and read [who health sharing is wrong for](/p/who-health-sharing-is-wrong-for/) first.

No. There's a $500 member commitment per health event instead, and it resets per event rather than per year.

Yes, and it's published by age and household size. That cap is the direct answer to the usual objection that crowdfunding has no out-of-pocket maximum. On the monthly side, it does.
