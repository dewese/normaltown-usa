# What Maryland's Warning About CrowdHealth Actually Says

> Maryland's insurance regulator put out a notice about CrowdHealth. It doesn't say CrowdHealth is a scam. It says CrowdHealth isn't insurance, nobody is legally required to pay your bill, and if that goes wrong, the state can't help you. All three of those are true, and my family joined anyway. Here's the honest version of why, and who should read that notice and walk away.

If you've been looking into CrowdHealth, you may have run across something that stopped you cold: the Maryland Insurance Administration, the state agency that regulates insurance in Maryland, published a consumer advisory with CrowdHealth's name in the title. **[VERIFY: date]**

That's the kind of thing that makes you close the tab.

I'm a CrowdHealth member. My family of four has used it since 2022. And I think you should read that advisory. Not skim it. Read it. So let me walk you through what it actually says, what it doesn't say, and what my family decided.

## First, what it is not

It is not a fraud alert. It is not an enforcement action. Nobody got shut down, fined, or sued. **[VERIFY: confirm no enforcement action accompanied it]**

A consumer advisory is a state agency saying "here is a thing that exists, and here is what you should understand about it before you hand over money." That's a different animal from "this company is breaking the law."

## What it actually says

Three things, as best I can read it: **[VERIFY all three against the source]**

**One. CrowdHealth is not insurance.** Maryland's point is that these arrangements don't meet the requirements for health coverage under Maryland law. Which is true, and which CrowdHealth says themselves, loudly, on their own website. They call themselves a healthcare crowdfunding platform. They are not hiding it.

**Two. It doesn't fit the religious exemption either.** Maryland allows healthcare sharing ministries to operate under certain conditions. Those are built around members sharing common religious or ethical beliefs. CrowdHealth is for-profit and secular, so it isn't one of those, and it doesn't get that carve-out.

**Three, and this is the one that matters most. If you don't get paid, the state can't help you.** When an insurance company denies your claim wrongly, you can file a complaint with your state insurance department and they have teeth. With CrowdHealth, there's no regulator standing behind you. If the crowd doesn't fund your bill, your recourse is the company's own process, and then nothing.

## Why that third one is the real point

I want to sit on this one, because it's the thing people skip.

When you buy insurance, you're buying two things. You're buying the money, and you're buying the **referee**. The referee is a state agency with the power to make a company pay. You never think about the referee until you need it, and most people never need it. But it's there.

CrowdHealth has the money part. A lot of bills get funded, quickly. What it does not have is the referee. There is no legal obligation for anybody to fund your bill, and no government office you can call if they don't.

That's not a loophole somebody found. That's the actual deal, stated in the actual terms, and it's the trade you're making. The costs are lower partly *because* that machinery isn't there.

## So why is my family still in it?

Three reasons, and I'll give you the weak spots in each one.

**The track record is strong.** A very large share of submitted bills get funded. **[VERIFY: use the confirmed number from the fact checklist, likely the 53,877 funded / 32 unfunded figure or the 99.8% figure, whichever is confirmed]** That's a real number and it's better than I expected when I started digging.

*The weak spot:* a track record is not a guarantee, and I won't describe it as one. It describes what happened before, with a smaller and different group of members than the one you'd be joining. It is evidence. It is not a promise.

**We can absorb the worst case.** We have savings. If a bill didn't get funded, it would hurt and it would not wreck us. That's a specific fact about my family's balance sheet, not a general argument.

*The weak spot:* this reason doesn't transfer to you at all. If a large unfunded bill would end you, the math is completely different and the answer is probably no.

**The incentives point the right way.** The company makes its money on a flat fee, not on denying your care. I wrote about why that matters in [Nobody Gets Paid to Make You Well](/p/nobody-gets-paid-to-make-you-well/).

*The weak spot:* good incentives are not the same as a legal obligation. It means nobody profits from stiffing you. It doesn't mean somebody has to pay you.

## Who should read the advisory and walk away

I'd rather you not join than join and get hurt, so here's the honest list.

- **You live in a state with an individual mandate.** Maryland is only one piece of this. California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Rhode Island, Vermont and DC have their own rules and possible penalties. **[VERIFY: current list]** Check yours before you cancel anything.
- **You have an ongoing condition you need covered.** The pre-existing rules are strict, and the whole point for you would be coverage that is guaranteed. Go read [who health sharing is wrong for](/p/who-health-sharing-is-wrong-for/).
- **An unfunded bill would ruin you.** If you don't have a cushion, you're buying a lower monthly cost with risk you can't actually carry.
- **You need the referee.** Some people, completely reasonably, want a regulator they can call. That's not a character flaw, it's a preference about risk, and insurance is the product that sells it. Buy the insurance.
- **You'd lose a big ACA subsidy.** If your subsidy makes a marketplace plan cheap, there may be no savings here at all. Run the actual numbers first.

If any of those is you, I'm glad the advisory made you stop.

## What I'd do if I were you

Read the advisory yourself. The link is at the bottom. Don't take my summary for it, and don't take CrowdHealth's rebuttal for it either.

Then ask yourself one question: **if a $40,000 bill did not get funded, what happens to my family?**

If the answer is "it'd be painful but we'd survive," then you're in the group this can work for, and you should go read [what CrowdHealth doesn't cover](/p/what-crowdhealth-doesnt-cover/) next.

If the answer is "we'd lose the house," stop here. Buy insurance. Nothing on this page is worth that risk, and no discount code changes that math.

## Questions I get about this

### Is CrowdHealth illegal in Maryland?

No. **[VERIFY]** The advisory is a warning to consumers, not a ban. It's telling Maryland residents what protections they would not have.

### Have other states done this?

**[VERIFY: check for other state notices before publishing]** Several states have individual mandates or extra steps that affect members, which is a separate issue from a formal advisory.

### Does CrowdHealth count as insurance for my taxes?

No. It is not minimum essential coverage under the ACA. There's no federal penalty for that since 2019, but some states have their own. Talk to a tax pro about your state.

### Does this change anything about your recommendation?

It changes how I say it, not what I say. I've always said it's not insurance and there's no guarantee. Maryland said the same thing with more authority than I have. If anything, I'm glad a regulator put it in writing, because it means fewer people join without understanding the trade.
