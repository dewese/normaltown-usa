# Normaltown USA — Version 2 Plan

> **Status:** proposal, 2026-09-21. Nothing here is built yet.
> **The one-sentence bet:** stop being a money blog that mentions CrowdHealth, and
> become the independent, member-run reference site for CrowdHealth, warm enough that
> a nervous 45-year-old trusts it and complete enough that Google prefers it to the
> company's own pages.

---

## 0. The headline findings first

Four things came out of the research that change what V2 should be. Read these before
anything else.

### 0.1 You have almost no content that targets the word "CrowdHealth"

51 published posts. 25 of them mention CrowdHealth in the body. Exactly **one** has
CrowdHealth in the title or the URL (`what-crowdhealth-doesnt-cover`). There is no
`/crowdhealth/` hub page at all.

You have been writing about **health sharing**, which is the category. People searching
with their wallet out are typing **CrowdHealth**, which is the brand. Those are two
different query spaces and you are only in one of them. This is the single biggest gap
between where the site is and what you asked for.

CrowdHealth itself does not use the phrase "health sharing" for its own product. Their own
copy calls it **"healthcare crowdfunding"** and explicitly distinguishes itself from health
sharing ministries (confirmed by David, 2026-09-21).

The fix is not a mass rename, and it is worth being clear about why. "Health sharing" is
the phrase readers actually type; "healthcare crowdfunding" has almost no consumer search
volume, because people searching "crowdfunding" are thinking GoFundMe. Renaming 18 slugs
to a phrase nobody searches would trade the site's category traffic for a vocabulary
argument. The rule instead: **CrowdHealth itself is healthcare crowdfunding; the category
the reader is shopping in is health sharing; bridge the two once where both appear.**

That bridge is a genuine differentiator. The big comparison sites file CrowdHealth under
health sharing ministries, which is the one thing it says it is not, and the Maryland
advisory turns on exactly that distinction.

### 0.2 The space is already taken, by sites built exactly the way you are describing

Every search I ran returned the same handful of domains, over and over:

- **whichhealthshare.com** is dominating. They own review pages, comparison pages,
  per-question answer pages (`/answers/cost`), a side-by-side `/compare` tool, and a
  first-person "I left health insurance for CrowdHealth" editor review. They appeared in
  five of six searches.
- **healthsharenerd.com** owns the "best health sharing ministry 2026" roundup.
- **gabrielcare.co**, **healthshare101.com** are running the same play.
- **Personal-brand reviews outrank most of them on trust queries**:
  Nat Eliason's "I Canceled My Family's Health Insurance," Chris Gimmer's CrowdHealth
  review, Emily Blasik's "Why My Family Switched," and a blogger at
  organicbeautylover.com with "My Brutally Honest CrowdHealth Review After One Year."

That last group is the good news. **The single-person, real-family, I-actually-did-this
review is beating the big comparison sites on the highest-intent queries.** That is
precisely the asset you already have and they do not: a real family of four, uninsured
since 2022, with four years of receipts. You are not late. You are just not aimed.

### 0.3 Some numbers currently on your site look out of date

This is urgent, because your entire pitch is "I tell you the honest version."

`pipeline/06-cta-affiliate.md` was last verified 2026-09-03 and feeds every post. Current
public sources disagree with it on several points:

| Your file says | What 2026 sources say | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 37,000+ members | ~15,000 to 17,000 members | Overstates by 2x |
| 45,000+ bills funded | 53,877 bills crowdfunded lifetime, 32 unfunded or partially funded | Understates the good number, and you are missing the *best* stat you have |
| $60/mo flat fee, variable contributions | $60 base, but contributions appear **capped around $140/mo per adult under 55**; typical all-in ~$140 to $200 | You are describing an uncapped cost when there may be a cap. The cap is a *selling point* you are not using |
| Pre-existing: ineligible yrs 1 to 2, then $25k/yr cap | At least one source describes a 6-month waiting period | Either the rules changed or the sources conflict |
| 4.9 Trustpilot, 1,000+ reviews | 4.6 in a 2026 review roundup | Small, but wrong numbers are wrong |
| Referral = 3 months at $99 | ~~Coupon sites advertise $99/mo for 6 months~~ | **RESOLVED 2026-09-21: three months is correct, the aggregators are wrong. No change needed** |

I could not verify these directly. `joincrowdhealth.com` and `whichhealthshare.com` are
both blocked by this environment's network proxy, so every figure above comes from search
result summaries, not from the source pages. **Treat this table as "go check these," not
as fact.** Verifying it is task #1 in the plan below.

### 0.4 Maryland issued a consumer advisory about CrowdHealth in March 2026

The Maryland Insurance Administration published a consumer advisory naming CrowdHealth
specifically. The substance of it: CrowdHealth is for-profit, does not require shared
religious beliefs (so it does not fit Maryland's healthcare sharing ministry exemption),
is not insurance, does not guarantee payment, and **if you do not get paid, no state
insurance regulator can help you.**

Your site does not mention this anywhere.

Two reasons that has to change:

1. **It is a search query.** People are going to google "crowdhealth maryland" and
   "is crowdhealth legal in my state." Right now somebody else answers that.
2. **It is the single best trust-building opportunity you will ever get.** A site that
   says "here is the state regulator warning about the thing I use, here is what it
   actually means, here is why my family stayed anyway, and here is who should walk away"
   is more believable than any amount of warm copy. The people who already found the
   advisory and got spooked are exactly the high-intent readers you want.

## 1. Strategy: what V2 actually is

### 1.1 The positioning, and one correction to your instinct

You said you were thinking about a "fan site" or "CrowdHealth-focused influencer."

Half right. The **focus** is right. The **fan** part is the wrong word, and in this niche
it is actively expensive:

- Health coverage is what Google calls a YMYL topic (Your Money or Your Life). Those pages
  are held to the highest quality bar in the guidelines. An enthusiastic booster site is
  the exact profile that gets buried.
- The queries that convert are skeptical queries. "Is CrowdHealth legit." "CrowdHealth
  complaints." "What if they don't pay." A fan site cannot rank for those, because a fan
  site cannot honestly answer them.
- Your own brand voice spec already got this right: *"A skeptic converts better than a
  hyped reader."* That is the whole business.

So the position is:

> **The independent, member-run CrowdHealth guide. Written by one family who actually
> uses it, who will tell you the parts the company won't.**

Not a fan. A **long-term member who keeps receipts.** The warmth comes from the person,
not from cheerleading. Nat Eliason and Chris Gimmer are outranking venture-funded
comparison sites on this exact energy.

A required piece of that: a permanent, visible line stating you are **not affiliated with,
endorsed by, or employed by CrowdHealth.** It protects you on trademark, it is what the
FTC wants, and counterintuitively it is the line that makes the rest of the site more
believable, not less.

### 1.2 The three jobs V2 has to do

| Job | The reader's question | What wins it |
|---|---|---|
| **Be findable** | "crowdhealth review," "crowdhealth cost," "is crowdhealth legit" | Branded-query content that does not exist yet |
| **Be believable** | "why should I trust this guy" | Four years of real receipts, the Maryland advisory handled head-on, accurate numbers, a face |
| **Be warm** | "does this feel like a person or a funnel" | The look and feel overhaul in section 5 |

Right now the site does the middle one partially and the other two barely.

## 2. Workstream A — Trust and accuracy (do this first, it is cheap)

Nothing else matters if the numbers are wrong. Ship order:

**A1. Re-verify the CrowdHealth fact sheet.** Go through `pipeline/06-cta-affiliate.md`
line by line against the live member guide, pricing page, and the referral program page.
Fix the six rows in section 0.3. Put a `Last verified: YYYY-MM-DD` stamp on the file and
set a recurring monthly reminder to redo it. This file feeds every post you write, so an
error here is an error in fifty places.

**A2. Add a public "How I keep this accurate" page.** Short. What you verify, how often,
where the numbers come from, what you do when you find you were wrong. Link it from the
footer and from every CrowdHealth page. This is a real E-E-A-T signal and almost nobody
in this niche has one.

**A3. Write the Maryland advisory post.** Working title: *What Maryland's Warning About
CrowdHealth Actually Says.* Quote the advisory. Explain the mechanism plainly (there is no
regulator backstop, because it is not insurance). Say what your family concluded and why.
Say clearly who should read that advisory and walk away. Link to the primary source.

**A4. Build the state-by-state page.** VT, CA, MA, NJ, RI and DC have individual mandates
or extra steps. Maryland has now issued an advisory. Other states may follow. One page,
one table, updated as things change. This is a durable ranking asset and nobody owns it.

**A5. Add the standing non-affiliation line.** Site-wide, in the footer, plus at the top
of the CrowdHealth hub.

**A6. Move the affiliate disclosure above the link, not below it.** Your partner card
currently puts the disclosure under the button. FTC guidance is that disclosure should be
unavoidable *before* the click. Small change, real protection, and it reads as more honest
to a human too.

**A7. Publish a "what I earn" page.** The actual number. $250 per member who stays 3
months. What you do and do not get paid for. Why you still recommend it. Doctor of Credit
built a whole reputation on this kind of transparency while competitors hid it. On a site
whose only product is trust, showing the money is the strongest move available.

## 3. Workstream B — The CrowdHealth hub (the structural fix)

Right now a person who lands on any post has to figure the site out from a
reverse-chronological blog. Build the thing they actually want.

### 3.1 `/crowdhealth/` — the pillar page

One long, genuinely complete page. Target query: **"crowdhealth review," "crowdhealth,"
"how does crowdhealth work."** This becomes the most important URL on the site.

Structure:

1. **The 60-second answer box.** What it is, what it costs, who it is right for, who it is
   wrong for. Above the fold, before anything else.
2. **Our four years, in numbers.** Real family-of-four data. What you have paid, what you
   have submitted, what got funded, how long it took, what did not get funded. This is the
   section nobody can copy and the reason the page ranks.
3. **What it costs**, with a real worked example, including the contribution cap.
4. **How a bill actually gets paid**, step by step with your own timeline.
5. **What it does not cover.** Full list, no softening.
6. **Who should not join.** Named, specific.
7. **The regulator question**, linking the Maryland post.
8. **How it compares** to insurance, to a high-deductible plan, to COBRA, to the other
   sharing plans.
9. **FAQ**, driven by the question list in section 4.
10. **The referral offer**, last, after all of the above, with the disclosure above it.

Then link every one of the 25 existing CrowdHealth posts into it as the supporting
cluster, and link them back to the hub. That internal linking is most of the SEO work.

### 3.2 The sub-pages that hang off it

- `/crowdhealth/cost/` — the pricing calculator page
- `/crowdhealth/vs/` — the comparison index, with a child page per competitor
- `/crowdhealth/reviews/` — what real members say, sourced and attributed, never invented
- `/crowdhealth/faq/` — the question index
- `/crowdhealth/discount/` — the offer page (section 6)

### 3.3 Two interactive tools

Static pages get read. Tools get **linked to**, which is how you earn the authority that
makes everything else rank.

- **The cost calculator.** Age, family size, current premium, current deductible. Outputs
  the honest side-by-side including the worst case where the crowd does not fund. Put the
  worst case in by default. It converts better and it is true.
- **The "should I switch" quiz.** Five to seven questions. It must be willing to output
  "no, stay on insurance" and mean it. That single honest outcome is worth more than the
  signups it costs you.

Both are pure static HTML plus a little JavaScript, which your build already supports.

## 5. Workstream D — Look and feel (the "it's cold" problem)

You are right, and it is not only the black.

### 5.1 What is actually causing it

| Cause | Why it reads cold |
|---|---|
| `#0F0F0F` canvas | Near-black backgrounds read as tech, crypto, luxury. Not kitchen table |
| `#2DD4FF` electric cyan | This is a crypto and SaaS accent. It is the single most "startup" thing on the page |
| Black and white family photo | A B&W portrait is editorial and distant. A color snapshot is a neighbor |
| Manrope everywhere | Clean geometric sans. Neutral at best, techy in volume. No human warmth in the letterforms |
| Hard-edged tiles, uppercase micro-labels, offset cyan shadows | The whole Visualize-Value vocabulary. Great for bitcoin explainers, wrong for a mom deciding about her kid's healthcare |

The current identity is beautifully consistent. It is just consistent with a different
business than the one you are building.

### 5.2 The V2 direction

The 2026 design research points the same way for exactly this problem: warmth is replacing
cool minimalism, and deep teal plus warm earth tones is the combination that reads
trustworthy, premium and human at the same time. Off-black is fine; pure cold black plus
electric accent is what dates a site.

Proposed palette, as a starting point to react to:

| Token | Now | Proposed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `--canvas` | `#0F0F0F` | `#FBF8F3` warm paper | Light, warm, readable. The biggest single change |
| `--ink` | `#FFFFFF` | `#1B1917` warm near-black | Text that is warm, not clinical |
| `--accent` | `#2DD4FF` cyan | `#0E6F63` deep teal | Trust without the crypto signal. Passes contrast on the warm canvas |
| `--warm` | none | `#C96A3E` clay | For highlights, pull quotes, "in short" boxes |
| `--panel` | dark gray | `#F2ECE3` | Soft card fill instead of a darker hole |

Keep a dark mode, and keep the current dark palette **as** the dark mode. Nothing is
wasted. You could also keep cyan alive as the bitcoin-section accent, which turns the
existing identity into a section color rather than the whole brand.

### 5.3 Type

Pair a warm serif for headlines with your existing sans for body. Fraunces, Bitter or
Source Serif 4 all say "a person wrote this." Keep Manrope for body and UI. A serif
headline over a warm background does more for the "personable and trusted" goal than any
copy change.

### 5.4 The human elements

These matter more than the palette:

- **Swap the family portrait to color.** The B&W is the coldest object on the site.
- **Add real, casual photos.** Not a studio headshot. You at the kitchen table, the actual
  CrowdHealth app on your actual phone, a real bill.
- **Handwriting or signature** at the end of posts, under "David."
- **Show your face in the "in short" box.** The avatar is already there. Make it bigger.
- **Short video or voice.** Even a 90-second phone video on the hub page saying "hi, here
  is who I am and why I wrote this" moves believability more than a month of copy.
- **Loosen the geometry.** Softer corners, more rounded cards, fewer uppercase
  letter-spaced labels.

### 5.5 Tone

The voice spec is already good and already warm. The gap is that the *site chrome* is
colder than the writing. Two copy changes worth making:

- The tagline `Money & Healthcare Made Simple` is a category description. It is the
  coldest sentence on the site. Your own `homepage-copy.md` already proposed better:
  *Keep more of the money you already make.* Even warmer for V2 positioning, something
  closer to: *We dropped our health insurance in 2022. Here's everything we learned.*
- Lead with the family, not the topic.

## 6. Workstream E — Conversion and referral mechanics

### 6.1 The offer is fine. The alarm was false.

**Resolved 2026-09-21.** Member referral discounts are three months, full stop. The
"$99/month for six months" on the coupon aggregators is wrong or stale, so the site's copy
was right and nothing needs changing. Those aggregators are not a source and should never
be cited as one.

Other influencer codes are still live and indexed (BLUE, WOODS, KETOSNACKZ), so those
pages keep leaking commission on branded searches. That is what section 6.2 is for.

Still worth a conversation, just not as a fix: ask CrowdHealth what a **partner**
arrangement looks like, as distinct from the standard member referral every member gets.
They have a "Manager, Crowd and Influencer Marketing" role posted, so there is a person
whose job is exactly this, and a site with 50+ posts, four years of member history and a
ranking hub page is a different proposition from a member texting a code to a friend.

### 6.2 Own "crowdhealth promo code"

Generic coupon sites currently rank for this and they are strip-mining your commission
with codes they scraped. Build `/crowdhealth/discount/` that actually answers it: what the
current offer really is, what the codes floating around are worth, what the catch is, what
expired. An honest page beats a scraped one, and it is a high-intent query with no real
competition.

### 6.3 Stop losing the traffic you already have

The site has 51 posts and no way to stay in touch beyond a beehiiv footer embed. Add:

- **A real lead magnet** tied to the hub: "The 12 questions to ask before you cancel your
  insurance," as a PDF or a simple email course. Gate it lightly or not at all.
- **A sticky, warm CTA on CrowdHealth posts** that is not the same footer card as
  everywhere else.
- **Track the referral.** Add UTM tags to every instance of the referral link so you know
  which post actually earns. Right now every link is identical and you cannot tell.

### 6.4 Diversify off the single affiliate

$250 per signup on one partner is a good business and a fragile one. If CrowdHealth
changes the program, the site's revenue goes to zero. Worth planning: direct consulting
(you already offer it, but it has no page), a paid "switching kit," and the other health
sharing plans as secondary affiliates for the readers you honestly turn away. That last
one is the elegant version, because it means the "no, don't join CrowdHealth" answer still
has somewhere useful to send people.

## 7. Workstream F — Getting found off-site

Rankings in a YMYL niche need signals from outside your own domain.

- **Be findable as a person.** The named-person reviews outranking the big sites all have
  an audience somewhere else first. YouTube is the obvious one for this topic. A single
  honest 10-minute "our four years on CrowdHealth" video would rank on its own and feed
  the site.
- **Be present where the question gets asked.** Reddit, the CrowdHealth community,
  Facebook groups for self-employed and early retirees. Answer questions genuinely, link
  only when it is the actual answer, and disclose every time. Dumping affiliate links in
  forums will get you banned and is not the play.
- **Get on the comparison sites' radar.** whichhealthshare.com and healthsharenerd.com
  need real member data. You have four years of it. A quoted source link from them is
  worth more than ten guest posts.
- **Keep the AI crawlers welcome.** Your `robots.txt` and `llms.txt` setup is already
  ahead of the field here. As more of this research happens inside AI assistants, being
  the cleanest, most citable source on CrowdHealth is a real channel. Lean into it: the
  answer boxes, the sources discipline and the FAQ schema you already generate are exactly
  what gets cited.
