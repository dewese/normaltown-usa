## First, the thing almost everybody gets wrong

CrowdHealth is **not a health sharing ministry**, and it doesn't call itself health sharing at all. Its own term is **healthcare crowdfunding**.

That sounds like hair-splitting. It isn't, and here's why it matters to you:

- A health sharing ministry is built around members who share religious or ethical beliefs. Many states carve out a legal exception for them on that basis. CrowdHealth is for-profit and secular, so it doesn't get that carve-out.
- Most of the big comparison sites file CrowdHealth under "health sharing plans" anyway, which is the one thing it says it is not. If a review lumps it in with the faith-based programs, that review doesn't understand the product.

I still use the phrase "health sharing" on this site, because that's what people type into Google when they're shopping. But when we're talking about CrowdHealth specifically, the accurate word is crowdfunding.

## What it costs

Two numbers, and then the one almost nobody tells you.

**One. A flat $60 a month, per person.** Children included, so a family of four pays $240. That's the subscription. It covers the app, the bill negotiators, and a Care Advocate who is a real human being. It does not change based on how sick you are.

**Two. A $500 member commitment per health event.** A health event is one thing that happens to you, like a broken arm or an appendix coming out. You cover the first $500. The community covers the rest of the eligible bill. This replaces a deductible, and it resets per event rather than per year.

**Three, and this is the one that should decide it for you: the monthly crowd contribution is hard-capped.**

On top of the $60, you chip in each month to fund other members' bills. That amount moves around. But it cannot exceed a published ceiling:

|---|---|---|

| Ages 55 to 64 | $280 | **$340 per person** |

That last row is the one people don't believe the first time. A family of four pays $240 in subscription and can be asked for at most $420 more. **Six hundred sixty dollars is the worst month that family can have**, before the $500 if somebody actually gets hurt.

And that's the ceiling, not the normal month. CrowdHealth has historically not asked for the full amount.

### Why the cap is the part that should convince you

The strongest argument against healthcare crowdfunding, the one I take seriously, is this: insurance gives you an out-of-pocket maximum, a real number you cannot go past. Crowdfunding gives you a promise and a track record.

On the monthly side, that argument is simply wrong. There is a maximum. It's published. You can do the arithmetic before you hand over a dollar.

I'm not going to tell you the cap makes the rest of the risk disappear. It doesn't. There's still no legal guarantee a big bill gets funded, and that's the real trade. But anybody who tells you this has no ceiling hasn't read the terms.

## How a bill actually gets paid

1. You get care. You tell them you're self-pay, which matters, because [cash prices are usually lower than insured prices](/p/why-cash-prices-beat-insured-prices/).
2. You submit the bill in the app.
3. CrowdHealth's team reviews it for eligibility and negotiates it down. Self-pay discounts commonly run 25 to 85 percent off billed charges.
4. You cover your $500 commitment for that event.
5. The remainder goes to the crowd. Members get two days to approve or deny each request.
6. The money goes directly to you or the provider. There's no pooled fund sitting in the middle.

The longer version, with a real example, is in [how a big hospital bill gets paid without insurance](/p/how-a-big-hospital-bill-gets-paid-without-insurance/).

## Conditions you already have

This is where most people's answer gets decided, so I'm giving you the actual rules rather than a summary.

CrowdHealth's term is **Previously Documented, Diagnosed, or Symptomatic** conditions. Anything diagnosed, documented, symptomatic, suspected, or treated **within five years of joining** falls under it.

- **Years one and two: not eligible for funding at all.**
- **Year three onward: a $25,000 per year crowdfunding cap** for those conditions.

Their own examples, and they say the list isn't exhaustive: asthma, congenital conditions (except children who join at birth), diabetes, genetic disorders, heart conditions, sleep apnea.

**High cholesterol and high blood pressure are explicitly exempt.** Those two don't count against you. I'm calling that out because they're the most common thing a 45-year-old is quietly worried about, and almost every write-up I've read leaves the exemption out.

If you have a real ongoing condition, read that again, slowly. Two years of nothing, then a $25,000 ceiling, is a very different product from insurance. For a lot of people it's disqualifying, and it should be.

## The regulator question

This is the honest heart of it, so I'm not going to soften it.

When you buy insurance, you're buying two things: the money, and the **referee**. The referee is a state agency with the power to make a company pay. You never think about it until you need it, and most people never need it.

CrowdHealth has the money part, with the track record above. It does not have the referee. There is no legal obligation for anybody to fund your bill, and there's no government office to call if they don't.

That's not a loophole somebody found. It's the actual deal, stated plainly in the actual terms, and it's the trade you're making. The costs are lower partly *because* that machinery isn't there.

Maryland's insurance regulator [published a consumer advisory about CrowdHealth](https://insurance.maryland.gov/Consumer/Pages/ConsumerAdvisory-MIA-warns-of-risks-involved-in-crowd-funding-healthcare-payment-platform-CrowdHealth.aspx) in March 2026 making essentially this point. I think you should read it yourself rather than take my summary or CrowdHealth's. I'm writing a full piece on it, and a state-by-state page, and I'd rather publish those late and right than fast and wrong.

Some states also have their own insurance mandates that affect you, and my list of which ones is out of date, so check your own state before you cancel anything.

## Who should not join

I'd rather you skip this than join and get hurt. Don't join if:

- **You have an ongoing condition you need covered.** See the rules above. Two years of nothing, then a $25,000 cap, is not coverage for a chronic illness.
- **An unfunded bill would ruin you.** No cushion means you're buying a lower monthly cost with risk you can't actually carry.
- **You need the referee.** Some people, completely reasonably, want a regulator they can call. That isn't a character flaw, it's a preference about risk, and insurance is the product that sells it. Buy the insurance.
- **You'd lose a big ACA subsidy.** If a subsidy makes a marketplace plan cheap for you, there may be no savings here at all. Run your actual numbers.
- **You live in a state with an individual mandate** and haven't checked what it costs you.

I wrote a whole post on this: [who health sharing is wrong for](/p/who-health-sharing-is-wrong-for/). It's the one I'd want a friend to read first.

## Questions I get about this

### Is CrowdHealth insurance?

No, and it never claims to be. It's a healthcare crowdfunding platform. It does not satisfy the ACA's minimum essential coverage requirement, and it isn't regulated as insurance, which means no state insurance department can step in on your behalf.

### Is CrowdHealth legit?

It's a real, operating company with a long public record: 53,877 bills funded, 99.8 percent of submissions funded, 4.9 stars on Trustpilot. Legit and insurance are different questions, though. It's legitimately what it says it is, which is a crowdfunding community with no legal guarantee.

### How much does CrowdHealth cost per month?

$60 per person, children included, plus a capped crowd contribution. Worst case is $200 a month under 55, $340 from 55 to 64, and $660 for a family of four or more. The crowd typically asks for less than the cap.

### Does CrowdHealth cover pre-existing conditions?

Not in years one and two, and then up to $25,000 a year from year three, for anything documented, diagnosed, symptomatic, suspected, or treated within five years of joining. High cholesterol and high blood pressure are exempt from those limits.

### What happens if the crowd doesn't fund my bill?

You're responsible for it. That's the trade. The funding rate is 99.8 percent, but a rate is not a guarantee and I won't describe it as one. [The longer answer is here](/p/what-if-the-crowd-doesnt-fund-your-bill/).

### Does CrowdHealth count as insurance on my taxes?

No. It isn't minimum essential coverage. There's no federal penalty for that anymore, but some states have their own mandates. Talk to a tax professional about your state.
